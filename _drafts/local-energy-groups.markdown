*Adapted from my 2023 policy memo on Virtual Power Plants and my 2024 ESPM 260 paper on Local Energy Groups, revisited in September 2026.*

If you own a house, have a suitable roof, and can afford solar and a battery, you can start to change your relationship with electricity. Generate some of it yourself, store it for later, perhaps get paid to help the grid. If you rent the apartment next door, you might use the same neighborhood grid and experience the same outages, but you don't get to put equipment on the roof.

That was one of the problems behind my work on Local Energy Groups. There's a lot of enthusiasm about turning consumers into participants in the energy system. But how much of that participation depends on already owning property?

In 2023, I wrote a policy memo arguing that PG&E should expand Virtual Power Plants, or VPPs, to help meet its capacity needs. A VPP coordinates smaller resources like home batteries, EV chargers, and thermostats. During a hot evening, batteries could discharge while some cars delay charging until later. Across enough households, those actions become useful to the grid. ([Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/edf/virtual-power-plants-projects))

Some of the equipment was already there. We could get more use out of it while new power plants and storage projects were being developed, and people would get paid for participating. But even a successful VPP doesn't necessarily change who owns the equipment or who has a say. The renter next door might benefit from a more reliable grid while still having no stake in the assets.

In my 2024 paper, I explored shared solar and storage that communities could own and govern. I called these Local Energy Groups (LEGs). The proposal brought together several ambitions: broader ownership, lower costs, local investment, and resilience during outages.

Those don't automatically come together, though. A project could give residents more control and still cost more. It could save money while providing no backup power. The question worth working through is whether shared ownership could give people benefits and influence they wouldn't otherwise have, and what it'd cost to make that happen.

## Some of the pieces already exist

California's Community Choice Aggregators, or CCAs, give communities a way to make electricity procurement decisions through public agencies. Ava Community Energy procures electricity while [PG&E continues delivering it](https://avaenergy.org/your-energy-options/how-it-works/). Ava's board includes local elected officials, and it has public meetings and a Community Advisory Committee. Those are real ways for residents to influence decisions. ([Ava governance](https://avaenergy.org/about-ava/governance/))

My paper was too dismissive of that arrangement. Being represented through a public agency is different from being a member-owner of a project, but the difference doesn't establish that one is better. We'd need to look at which decisions residents can influence and whether that influence changes anything for them.

These models overlap, too. Since my paper, Ava has announced a broader [VPP strategy](https://avaenergy.org/news/ava-announces-virtual-power-plant-initiative/), and its [SmartHome Battery program](https://avaenergy.org/go-electric/residential-solar-storage/smarthome-battery/residents/) offers installation rebates and participation payments. A LEG would have to offer something beyond giving this combination another name.

One example I looked at was Hepburn Energy in Australia, a [member-owned cooperative with a wind farm](https://www.hepburnenergy.coop/about/). Each member gets one vote, regardless of how many shares they hold, and members can receive returns when financially prudent. ([Hepburn membership](https://www.hepburnenergy.coop/membership/)) You don't need a turbine in your yard to own part of it.

That's a useful possibility for renters. It shows how ownership can be separated from the property where the equipment goes. It doesn't, by itself, tell us whether a similar arrangement in California would deliver cheaper electricity or include people who can't afford to buy shares.

## What would members actually get?

Imagine an apartment building, some houses with solar, and a community center. A Local Energy Group could own panels on a suitable shared roof and a battery at the center. Residents could become members, including through subsidized memberships for people who couldn't afford to invest. Households with existing batteries could contract to contribute some capacity. An operator would coordinate the equipment, while members set the rules for investment and sharing benefits.

That gives us something more specific to evaluate. Who puts up the money? Who gets paid, who gets a vote, and who bears a loss?

In the paper, I described keeping more energy revenue in the community and using it to fund other local priorities. But there's a step missing between collecting revenue and having money to reinvest. The group would still have to pay for financing, maintenance, insurance, administration, equipment replacement, and the grid services it uses. Those costs don't disappear when ownership changes.

A renter's share of the returns might be useful. Or it might be very small once everything's paid for. We'd need to compare it with what the same funding could achieve through an existing program, including one that subsidizes participation without asking the household to take investment risk. Bill savings, a return on an investment, and a benefit to the wider grid are different things; we'd need to account for each without counting the same value twice.

The incentives also need a more careful reading than I gave them in the paper. California's net billing tariff, often called NEM 3.0, does encourage storage: storing solar can be more valuable than exporting it immediately. Berkeley Lab's 2024 report found that around 60% of residential solar installations under net billing were paired with batteries, compared with around 10% under NEM. ([Tracking the Sun, 2024](https://emp.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-08/Tracking%20the%20Sun%202024_Report.pdf)) An incentive to buy a battery still doesn't give someone the money or space to install it. That's the access problem a shared project might help solve.

And access to ownership isn't the whole governance problem. One-member-one-vote sounds straightforward until some members contribute most of the capital, others need subsidies, and only a few have time to attend meetings. A tenant might receive a formal vote while the landlord controls the roof and the project developer controls the information.

The group would need rules for admitting members, allocating benefits to tenants, and letting people leave when they move. Someone would need to handle complaints. We'd also need to know what happens to members' money and the equipment if the organization fails. Standard contracts could help, but calling the organization a cooperative doesn't settle these choices.

There should still be room to hire expertise. Members could decide how much revenue to reinvest without voting on every battery dispatch. The harder question is how they hold the operator accountable without participation becoming another unpaid job.

## How local does it need to be?

My original proposal considered organizing around substations, so the resources would have a relationship to the grid serving them. That could make sense where a group can relieve a specific constraint. But a neighborhood boundary doesn't necessarily match a distribution circuit, and two batteries nearby on a map might not help the same overloaded equipment.

For ownership, the boundary could be different again. Residents could collectively invest in a larger project elsewhere. Would they get better returns? Would they still feel involved? If local generation costs more, what additional benefit would justify paying for it?

Backup power might be one reason, but we'd have to build it explicitly. A properly equipped battery at the community center could keep that building running during an outage. It wouldn't automatically power members' apartments. Supplying several buildings while disconnected from the wider grid requires the electrical arrangements and controls for intentional islanding, as in a [microgrid](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/solar-integration-distributed-energy-resources-and-microgrids-basics). Joining a VPP doesn't create those connections.

There's a tradeoff even at a single building. Energy discharged to earn revenue this evening won't still be in the battery if an outage starts before it can recharge. Keeping a larger reserve means less energy available to sell. [Tesla's California DSGS program](https://www.tesla.com/support/energy/virtual-power-plant/dsgs) lets participants set an event backup reserve, but a shared battery would require a shared decision about whose needs take priority. How much backup, for which loads, and at what cost?

The utility would still have a substantial role. My paper described utilities maintaining the wires while local groups used them, but “renting the wires” leaves a lot unresolved. Who authorizes dispatch when the circuit is constrained? Who pays for an upgrade that benefits some members more than others? Who remains responsible when something fails?

Software could help coordinate the equipment and account for payments. We'd still need to settle those responsibilities. Shared ownership doesn't inherently require a peer-to-peer trading market or a blockchain; ordinary contracts and accounting might be enough for the arrangement we're trying to build.

So a LEG could end up looking less like an independent neighborhood energy system and more like a cooperative project working with a CCA and a professional operator. Perhaps that's enough. The assets wouldn't all have to be nearby, and backup could be a separate service where it's worth the expense.

The comparison would be with a well-designed CCA or VPP program that already tries to include renters and lower-income households. Could shared ownership give those residents a meaningful additional benefit and an enforceable say in decisions, at a cost they and the wider system can support? If it couldn't, we'd need to change the arrangement. If it could, we'd have a reason to build it that goes beyond the appeal of making energy local.
