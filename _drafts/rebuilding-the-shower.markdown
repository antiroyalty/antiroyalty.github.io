We are rebuilding the shower in our Berkeley home. Demolition has not started, but the design work has already changed how I understand showers.

This post documents the decisions we have made so far and the [interactive model I built to test them]({{ '/shower-design/' | relative_url }}). It is a progress report, not a set of construction instructions. I want it to capture the work while the open questions are still visible, instead of waiting until the finished tile makes every decision look obvious.

## The mold we can see

About eight months ago, I noticed dark patches near the bottom of the shower walls, beside the linear drain. At first, I thought I had a surface-cleaning problem. I cleaned the stone with a steam cleaner. The patches came back.

The stranger part is that the growth does not seem to sit only on the grout or caulk. It appears within the face of the stone tile, as if it is coming from below. We had two contractors inspect it. Busk Bros, the contractor we plan to work with on the rebuild, confirmed that it is mold growing through the tile. The contractor who built the original shower about 10 years ago reached the same conclusion. After they both confirmed this, it became clear this was no longer a cleaning problem.

The spotting is not limited to the wall or the drain edge. It also appears across the faces of several stone tiles near the middle of the shower floor.

That wider pattern makes an isolated caulk problem less convincing. It is consistent with persistent moisture in the porous stone or in the setting bed below it. The photo cannot tell us which layer is wet or where the drainage path failed.

Our working theory is that water is staying trapped in the traditional shower base. In this type of shower, some water passes through the tile and grout into the mortar bed. A sloped layer below the liner should direct that water to secondary openings in the drain called weep holes. [Oatey's installation guidance](https://www.oatey.com/faqs-blog-videos-case-studies/blog/shower-installation-and-waterproofing-tips-common-errors) explains that a missing pre-slope or blocked weep holes can prevent the water from leaving, oversaturate the mortar bed, and contribute to discoloration, odor, mold, or mildew.

That mechanism fits what we can see, but it does not identify the exact defect. The weep holes may be plugged, covered, or otherwise unable to drain. The shower may also be missing the required pre-slope, or the drain and liner may not have been connected correctly. Demolition has not started, and I do not want to turn a plausible explanation into a diagnosis before we inspect the assembly.

We also looked underneath the shower from the crawl space and found no visible water damage. That is reassuring, but it does not explain the mold and does not prove that the shower assembly is sound.

What I know now is that a surface repair would not answer the question I care about: where is the water going? To answer that, we had to stop thinking only about tile and look at the whole assembly behind it.

During demolition, I want us to document:

- whether the mortar bed is wet or has a musty odor below the tile;
- whether there is a pre-slope below the liner;
- whether the weep holes are present, open, and protected from mortar;
- how the liner connects to the linear drain;
- how far the wet or discolored material extends from the drain; and
- whether the liner or wall-to-floor transitions have any penetrations or gaps.

## The real shower is behind the tile

The first thing I learned is that tile is not really the shower.

Tile is the surface we look at, touch, clean, and make design decisions about. But tile and grout are not, by themselves, the waterproof enclosure. The shower is the less photogenic assembly behind them: the sloped floor, drain connection, waterproof layer, seams, corners, penetrations, curb, and niche.

This sounds obvious after somebody explains it. It was not obvious to me when I started. I had been thinking about the shower as a tiled room. It is more accurate to think of it as a small water-management system that happens to have tile on top.

That distinction changed the order of our decisions. Before choosing tile orientation or grout color, we needed to decide where the water goes, what stops it from reaching the framing, and how all of the transitions become one continuous waterproof assembly.

## Why I am leaning toward a Schluter assembly

For this rebuild, I am leaning toward Schluter components because they are designed as parts of one bonded waterproofing system. “Better” is not quite the right universal word. There are well-built traditional showers and poorly built showers made from every brand of product. What appeals to me is that the critical connections are explicit.

The basic components we are considering are:

- **KERDI or KERDI-BOARD** for the waterproof surface behind the tile. KERDI is a membrane; KERDI-BOARD combines the backer and waterproofing layer.
- **KERDI-BAND** for seams and changes of plane, with **KERDI-KERECK** preformed pieces for the difficult inside and outside corners.
- **KERDI-LINE** for the linear drain and its integrated bonding flange.
- **KERDI-BOARD-SC** for the prefabricated foam curb.
- **KERDI-BOARD-SN or SNLT** for a prefabricated niche.

In Schluter's description of its [shower system](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/faq/shower-system-why-use), the waterproofing membrane sits directly below the tile and bonds to the drain at the top of the assembly. In a traditional shower-pan system, the liner sits below a mortar bed. With the bonded approach, the mortar bed does not have to become saturated before water reaches the waterproofing. Given the mold in our current shower and the possibility that water is trapped below the tile, I found that difference appealing.

The system also makes the fussy places easier to inspect. Seams require an overlap. Corners get preformed corner pieces. The drain has a surface that the membrane bonds to. The niche can arrive with sealed internal corners and a KERDI-BAND frame. It is still possible to install any of these things badly, but the intended path to a continuous waterproof layer is easier for me to understand.

It is also useful that “Schluter system” does not necessarily mean that every part of the shower has to be a prefabricated foam shape. The company's [bonded waterproofing guidance](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/article-tiled-shower-waterproofing) shows that the membrane and integrated drain connection can be used over either a mortar base or a foam tray. That matters for our unusually long 44-by-78-inch footprint and for the existing linear-drain plumbing. We still need the installer and Schluter to confirm the exact drain, adaptor, and plumbing transition in the field. Brand compatibility is not something I want to infer from a diagram.

My current major lesson is that continuity between the components is what helps prevent hidden failures. It is also what I want to be able to inspect this time.

## The six-inch curb that becomes a three-inch curb

The curb confused me at first. The standard KERDI-BOARD-SC curb is listed as **6 inches high and 4 1/2 inches wide**. Six inches sounded enormous. I pictured having to step over a little tiled wall every time I entered the shower.

But the stock curb is a starting blank, not a required finished height. Schluter says in its [curb-trimming instructions](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/faq/shower-system-kerdi-board-sc-trimming) that the height can be modified by cutting material from the bottom. The curb can also be shortened. Its width can be reduced by removing a center section, rejoining the remaining pieces with thin-set mortar or KERDI-FIX, and sealing the new seam with KERDI-BAND.

So the 6-inch-to-3-inch transition is not a different product. It is a field modification of the stock curb.

The planning sequence I now understand is roughly:

1. Establish the elevations of the finished bathroom floor and the finished shower floor, including the slope toward the drain.
2. Decide what “3 inches visible” means at the outside face and at the shower-side face. Those measurements may not be identical because the shower floor is sloped and the finish build-ups differ.
3. Work backward through the thickness of the tile and setting material to calculate the rough foam height.
4. Cut the required amount from the **bottom** of the curb so that its factory top remains intact.
5. Fully support and set the curb in thin-set mortar at the shower base.
6. Waterproof the curb-to-floor and curb-to-wall transitions with KERDI-BAND and the appropriate preformed corners, maintaining the required overlaps.
7. Make sure the finished top of the curb slopes inward toward the drain.
8. Tile it, and then verify the actual finished height.

The important caveat is that “I want a 3-inch curb” does not mean “cut the bare foam to exactly 3 inches.” The tile assembly has thickness, and the visible shower-side height depends on where the sloped floor meets the curb. The installer needs to calculate the cut from the finished elevations.

For our design, the curb will use the same 2-by-4-inch Cypress green tile as the shower floor. The pieces will remain in a vertical straight lay, with the short exposed edges bullnosed. That keeps the curb connected to the floor and green wall band instead of making it look like a separate stripe. The cap still needs its functional inward pitch, even if the grout lines make it look perfectly level from across the room.

One width question remains open. The stock [KERDI-BOARD-SC curb](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/Building-Panels/KERDI-BOARD-Panels/Schluter%C2%AE-KERDI-BOARD-SC/p/KERDI_BOARD-SC) is 4 1/2 inches wide before tile. If we decide that feels too deep, the manufacturer's cut-and-rejoin method gives us a path to narrow it. That is a waterproofing detail, not just a cosmetic trim, so it belongs in the installer plan.

Six inches, in other words, is the stock size. Three inches is the design target.

## The niche

Our first version was 36 inches wide, 24 inches high, and about 5 inches deep. In the rendering, it looked proportionate, but when compared for size in our home, it turned otu to be huge. It occupied 864 square inches of wall and pulled attention away from the tile.

We briefly reduced it to 27 by 13 by 5 inches, which immediately felt more like storage and less like architecture. From there, we arrived at the proportions of a larger prefabricated Schluter niche: **28 inches wide, 12 inches high, and 3 1/2 inches deep**. Its face is 336 square inches, about 61 percent smaller than the original 36-by-24 opening.

The shallower depth worried me at first because the existing niche is probably closer to 5 inches deep. But 3 1/2 inches fits the bottles we use, and depth is only useful if the things in the back remain easy to see and reach. The prefabricated niche also has a different kind of value: its interior corners are already sealed, it integrates with the surrounding KERDI or KERDI-BOARD, and it includes a KERDI-BAND frame for the perimeter. That removes several custom waterproofing transitions from a part of the shower that gets direct spray.

The current height in the design is:

- **48 inches from the finished shower floor to the bottom of the niche**
- **60 inches from the finished shower floor to the top**

For people who are 5 feet 7 inches and 5 feet 8 inches tall, that puts the contents below eye level but high enough that nobody needs to crouch. The bottom ledge is around chest height. This is not a code-derived number; it is an ergonomic choice. Before framing, I want to tape a 28-by-12 rectangle on the wall, put our actual bottles against it, and make sure it feels natural.

The interior will use the same Cypress green 2-by-4 tile in a vertical straight lay, including the returns. A bullnose lip will finish the exposed front edge. This turns the niche into one concentrated green detail instead of another large field of wall tile.

There is one product-selection detail to verify. Schluter's current [shower-system data sheet](https://assets.schluter.com/asset/570120892212/document_2nqfpg0pi13itfso529nhmlr7f/Shower%20System%20Data%20Sheet.pdf?content-disposition=inline) lists the standard unlighted KERDI-BOARD-SN as 12 inches wide by 28 inches high. The explicitly horizontal 28-by-12 KERDI-BOARD-SNLT model includes lighting. We have not chosen a final product. Before framing, we need to confirm whether the unlighted version can be installed horizontally or whether we need a different niche.

## Building the shower before building the shower

Once we had enough dimensions, I built a small [interactive 3D model of the shower]({{ '/shower-design/' | relative_url }}) in HTML and JavaScript. The goal was not photorealism. I wanted a tool that would make proportion mistakes obvious and let us have concrete conversations with the contractor.

The model uses the shower's real dimensions as its coordinate system: **78 inches long, 44 inches wide, and 132 inches high**. It has three tiled walls, an opening along one long side, a ceiling rain head, controls on the left wall, a linear drain, and the 3-inch visible curb.

The tile layouts are also modeled rather than represented by a single flat color. The cream Ann Sacks Alo Dust wall tile is laid horizontally in a 16-by-32-inch module. The Cypress green 2-by-4 tile is laid vertically on the shower floor, the 8-inch-high wall band, the curb, and inside the niche. Grout joints are represented at approximately 3/16 inch, so a change in orientation changes the visual rhythm instead of just changing a label.

### How the tool works

The page uses Three.js to draw the geometry in a browser. Inches are used directly as scene units, which makes dimensions much easier to reason about. A 28-inch-wide niche is literally 28 units wide in the scene. The camera can orbit around the shower, and preset buttons jump to useful views: the overall shower, a niche close-up, a curb close-up, and the wall trim.

Dimension markers are HTML labels projected from points in the 3D scene. This lets the measurements stay crisp and readable while the model rotates. It also means that the labels and the actual geometry are separate systems—which led to the most useful bug in the project.

### The flat-niche bug

In an early version, I changed the niche label from 3 1/2 inches deep to 5 inches deep. I moved the green back tile. From the front, it still looked flat.

The problem was that I had changed the nominal depth without changing the whole wall assembly. The cream wall tiles and backing plane still ran across the niche opening, so the back and side returns were visually collapsed behind a flat surface. The dimension label said one thing while the geometry said another.

Fixing it required treating the niche as an actual void:

- cut an opening through the wall backing, grout field, and cream tile field;
- move the niche back surface behind the wall plane by the full modeled depth;
- add visible top, bottom, left, and right tiled returns;
- add the bullnose lip at the wall face; and
- anchor the depth dimension from the wall face to the niche back, rather than positioning a floating label nearby.

The lesson was delightfully blunt: **labels are not geometry**. If a dimension matters, it has to exist in the construction of the model, not only in the annotation.

### What the model helped us decide

The model made several debates much easier:

- A 36-by-24-inch niche was too dominant; 28 by 12 felt proportional.
- The curb looked disconnected when its green tile ran horizontally. Returning it to the same vertical straight lay as the shower floor made the materials feel intentional.
- An 8-inch green band at the bottom of the three walls reads as a base, while a taller band begins to compete with the cream field tile.
- A 3-inch visible curb feels low and ordinary in the room, despite the fact that the stock foam curb begins at 6 inches.
- The niche needed to be modeled as a real recess before we could judge its depth.

The interactive model is now a standalone page that can be opened on a phone or laptop and shared with the contractor. It is one HTML file, although it loads the Three.js library from a CDN and therefore needs an internet connection. It is not part of the site's main navigation.

The model is a communication tool, not a construction drawing. It does not prove that the framing can accept the niche, that the plumbing transition is correct, that the slope meets requirements at every point, or that the selected trim pieces are available. Its job is to expose questions before tile is set. For that, it has already been more useful than a folder of inspiration photos.

## Decisions so far

- Shower footprint: 44 inches by 78 inches
- Ceiling height: 11 feet
- Three tiled walls, with the opening on a long side
- Linear drain, with the exact transition from the existing plumbing still to be confirmed in the field
- Approximately 3 inches of visible finished curb height, using a trimmed KERDI-BOARD-SC curb
- Ann Sacks Alo Dust 16-by-32-inch wall tile in a horizontal lay
- Cypress green 2-by-4-inch tile in a vertical straight lay on the floor, wall base, curb, and niche interior
- Prefabricated niche proportion: 28 inches wide by 12 inches high by 3 1/2 inches deep
- Niche bottom at 48 inches and top at 60 inches above the finished shower floor
- Ceiling-mounted rain shower head, with controls on the left wall

## What remains open

- What failed in the original shower and what demolition reveals
- Whether the framing can accept the niche we designed
- The final linear-drain and plumbing transition
- How the waterproofing was inspected and flood-tested
- The tile quantities we actually ordered, including waste and bullnose pieces
- What changed between the model and the finished shower
- Photos of the completed assembly, including the parts that disappear behind the tile

For now, the most important shift has been learning to see the shower in layers. The visible design still matters. But the prettier the tile, the more I want to know exactly what is holding back the water behind it.
