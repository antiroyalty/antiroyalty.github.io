We’re rebuilding the shower in our Berkeley home. It’s my first big house project, and we haven’t even started demolition yet. But there’s already been quite a bit to figure out! Including how a shower actually works, which turns out to involve much more than the tile you can see.

About eight months ago, I noticed dark patches near the bottom of the shower walls, beside the linear drain. I thought it was something I could clean. I used a steam cleaner on the stone, and the patches came back.

The strange part was where they appeared. The growth wasn’t just along the grout or caulk; it seemed to be coming through the face of the stone itself. There were also spots across several tiles in the middle of the floor, away from the wall and drain.

We had two contractors look at it: Busk Bros, who we’re planning to work with on the rebuild, and the contractor who built the original shower about ten years ago. Both said it was mold growing through the tile. So, unfortunately, more cleaning wasn’t going to solve this.

Our working theory is that water is getting trapped underneath. In a traditional shower base, some water passes through the tile and grout into the mortar bed below. There’s a waterproof liner underneath that, and the surface supporting it should slope toward the drain. Small openings called “weep holes” let that water escape. If those openings are blocked, or the liner doesn’t have the right slope underneath it, water can stay in the mortar bed. [Oatey’s explanation](https://www.oatey.com/faqs-blog-videos-case-studies/blog/shower-installation-and-waterproofing-tips-common-errors) describes how that can lead to persistent moisture, discoloration, and mold.

Which sounds consistent with what we’re seeing. But we don’t know yet whether the problem is blocked weep holes, a missing slope, the connection to the drain, or something else. We’ll need to take it apart.

We looked underneath the shower from the crawl space and didn’t see water damage. Our contractor doesn’t expect to find a major leak behind it, and thinks we may find some spotted material closer to the surface when the tile comes off. Hopefully that’s the extent of it. I’d still like to see how wet the mortar is, how the liner connects to the drain, and how far the affected material goes.

This was the first thing I had to get my head around: tile and grout aren’t the waterproofing. I’d been thinking about the shower as a tiled room. But the water that gets through that surface needs somewhere to go too.

So before we could settle on tile, I wanted to understand what would be underneath it this time.

I’m leaning toward a Schluter assembly, though we haven’t finalized the wall backer or waterproofing system. What appeals to me is that the pieces are designed to connect into one continuous waterproof layer. There’s the membrane or waterproof board, the bands covering seams, the pieces for corners, and a drain with a flange that the waterproofing bonds to.

With this approach, the waterproof membrane sits directly below the tile and setting material, above the shower base. That protects the base from becoming saturated during regular use. Given that trapped water may be what’s wrong with our current shower, this difference matters to me. [Schluter’s system explanation](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/faq/shower-system-why-use)

I also want to understand the connections well enough to look at them before they disappear. How does the wall waterproofing meet the floor? What happens at the curb? How do the corners inside the niche connect to everything around them? These are small places with a lot going on.

Using the system doesn’t mean we need a prefabricated shape for every part of the shower, either. Our footprint is unusually long, 44 by 78 inches, and we have existing linear-drain plumbing to work with. We can still use a custom base or frame a niche to fit the wall. The installer will need to confirm the exact drain and plumbing transition, and how those parts connect to the waterproofing.

Before any tile goes on, we’ll inspect the wall assembly and flood-test the base. Our contractor’s plan is to plug the drain, fill the base, mark the water level, and leave it overnight. Then check whether the level has dropped and look underneath for leaks, both while the water is sitting there and when it drains.

If something leaks, this is when I want to find out. We’ll still be able to see it and get to it.

The curb was another thing that confused me. The standard Schluter foam curb is six inches high. Six inches sounded enormous! I pictured stepping over a little tiled wall every time I got into the shower.

It turns out you can trim it. The six-inch dimension is the stock size, and Schluter allows material to be cut from the bottom to reduce its height. [Curb-trimming instructions](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/faq/shower-system-kerdi-board-sc-trimming)

We’re aiming for about three inches of visible finished curb. But that doesn’t mean cutting the foam to three inches, because tile and setting material add thickness, and the floors on either side don’t necessarily meet it at the same elevation. The shower floor slopes, too. The installer needs to work backward from the finished heights to figure out the cut.

Even “a three-inch curb” needed a more precise definition than I’d given it.

We’re planning to tile the curb in the same Cypress green 2-by-4-inch tile as the shower floor, with the same vertical straight lay and bullnosed exposed edges. In the model, changing the curb tile to run horizontally made it feel disconnected from the floor. Keeping the orientation consistent worked much better. The top will still need to slope inward so water drains into the shower.

Then there’s the drain placement. Our contractor suggested putting the linear drain along the short wall with the controls, but moving it two to four inches away from the wall. That leaves a narrow strip of tile behind it, which can slope toward the drain instead of letting water sit at the wall.

I chose two inches as the design target because it matches the short dimension of our floor tile and keeps that strip small. The contractor said the strip should slope a quarter inch per foot. Across two inches, that’s only about 1/24 inch of fall. A very small slope, but one that still has to be there.

In the model, I’m measuring the gap from the finished wall to the near edge of the visible drain frame. We’ll need to confirm that measurement with the actual drain assembly, because the part you see isn’t the full size of the thing being installed.

The niche took a few iterations.

Our first version was 36 inches wide, 24 inches high, and about five inches deep. It looked reasonable in the rendering. When we compared those dimensions for size at home, it was huge. That’s six square feet of opening in the wall, just for shower bottles.

We reduced it to 27 by 13 inches, then settled on a target of 28 by 14. That’s about 55% less area than the first version. It felt much more proportionate, and let the wall tile have more of the attention.

The current model shows a depth of 3½ inches, initially based on a prefabricated niche. I was concerned that would be too shallow, since our existing one is probably closer to five inches deep. But our bottles fit. And there’s only so much benefit to more depth if the things in the back become harder to see and reach.

Our contractor said there’s no particular need to use a prefabricated niche. At 28 inches wide, he’d need to frame around the studs and opening anyway. So we can decide the final depth once the wall is open and we know how much space there is. It still needs to be waterproofed into the rest of the wall, including all those inside corners.

We’ve placed the bottom at 48 inches above the finished shower floor and the top at 62 inches. We’re 5 feet 7 inches and 5 feet 8 inches tall, so that should keep the bottles below eye level without making us crouch. Before framing it, I want to tape the rectangle on the wall and put our actual bottles against it. The first version looked fine on a screen too.

The niche interior will use the same green tile as the floor and curb, including the sides, with a bullnose edge at the front.

A lot of these decisions became easier once I built an [interactive 3D model of the shower]({{ '/shower-design/' | relative_url }}). I wanted to be able to change dimensions, look at the result, and show the contractor what I meant.

The model uses the actual shower dimensions: 78 inches long, 44 inches wide, and 132 inches high. Three tiled walls, an opening along one long side, a ceiling rain head, and controls on the left wall. You can rotate it or jump to views of the niche, curb, and wall trim.

It’s built in HTML and JavaScript, using Three.js. I used inches directly as the scene units, so a 28-inch niche is 28 units wide. That made it easier to reason about the dimensions while changing things.

I also modeled the tile layout. We chose plain Florim SensiTerre Rosato porcelain for the walls, in the nominal 12-by-24-inch size, laid horizontally. The Cypress green 2-by-4 tile runs vertically on the floor, curb, inside the niche, and in an eight-inch band around the bottom of the three walls.

The grout joints are represented too, at approximately 3/16 inch. So changing tile orientation actually changes what you see. That helped with the curb, and with choosing the height of the green wall band. Eight inches felt like a base for the room; a taller band started competing with the Rosato tile.

The niche also needed to be an actual recess in the model before we could judge its depth. A rectangle on a wall can show you where something goes, but it doesn’t tell you much about reaching into it.

I can open the model on a phone or laptop and share it with the contractor. It’s already been more useful for these conversations than a folder of inspiration photos. We can point to the same thing and talk about a specific dimension or change.

There are still things it can’t settle. It doesn’t tell us what’s inside the wall, whether the drain connects to the existing plumbing, or exactly how the framing needs to work. Those questions are waiting for demolition. But it’s helped us work through the choices we *can* make now, including a few that looked good until we gave them real dimensions.

Before demolition, we also need a glass shop to remove the existing enclosure. We may be able to reuse it if the finished dimensions still work. That depends partly on the rebuilt curb and tile thickness, so we need to think about it now, while we still have the option.

The contractor estimates about two to two and a half weeks for the project. We’ll see what opening the shower does to that estimate. If the framing or subfloor needs repair, there’ll be more work before any of the new materials can go in.

For now, we have the tile choices, the model, and a much clearer idea of what we want to build. What I still want to know is why water has been staying in the old shower, and how much of it we’ll find when the tile comes off.
